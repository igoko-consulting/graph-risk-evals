# PRD — graph-risk-evals

**Document name:** When does a graph earn its cost in fraud and AML detection?
**Purpose:** Specify a public, reproducible lab that measures where graph methods start and stop paying for themselves in a regulated detection problem.
**Author:** Martin Cook
**Version:** Draft v1
**Change history:** 2026-11-19, MCook, first draft for review

**Status:** Draft. Several items are marked **Open** rather than resolved. The pre-registration in section 3 must be committed before the first result is generated, or the central claim of this repo is void.

---

## TL;DR

Organised fraud lives in the relationships between accounts, not in individual transactions, so per-transaction monitoring misses it by design. Graph methods are the standard response. They are also expensive to run, hungry for labels that arrive late and rarely, and harder to explain to an investigator or a regulator than the method they replace.

This lab measures that trade-off honestly on a published transaction graph. Four arms are built in increasing order of cost, from rules through graph-derived features to a graph neural network, each measured on the same time-aware split, at the same operating point, with a cost per decision attached. A separate test compares a graph store against a columnar store on traversal and aggregation workloads, to show that engine choice follows query shape.

The hypothesis is pre-registered. Whatever the result, it is reported.

## Context

Detection systems in payments mostly score one customer or one transaction at a time. A mule network defeats that by construction: every account behaves normally, and only the shape of the connections is wrong. Accounts opened in the same week, sharing devices and addresses, moving money through the same few exits.

Graph databases and graph machine learning exist to find that shape. The industry generally arrives at them after discovering the gap, often under regulatory pressure rather than as a planned investment.

The decision facing a product leader is not whether graphs can find rings. They can. It is whether the lift justifies the cost per decision, the label requirements and the loss of explainability, against a baseline that has been tuned properly rather than left weak for comparison.

Most published comparisons do not answer that, because the baseline is an afterthought and cost does not appear at all.

**Why this matters commercially.** In cross-border payments the margin kept per transaction is often well under one percent and is being compressed deliberately to win volume. Fraud losses, the analyst time spent on false positives and the compute running detection all draw on the same thin slice. A detection capability therefore has to pay for itself in basis points. That framing, not model architecture, is the product question.

## Goals and objectives

**Honesty.** The hypothesis is written down and committed before the first result. The finding is published whatever it is, including a null result.

**Comparability.** Every arm uses the same data, the same time-aware split, the same evaluation and the same operating point. Differences in score must come from method, not from setup.

**Economics.** Every arm carries a cost per decision, modelled and labelled as modelled, so lift is never reported alone.

**Explainability.** Every flagged entity produces an evidence chain a human can act on and a customer could appeal. Where a method cannot produce one, that is recorded as a finding.

**Reproducibility.** One command runs the pipeline end to end on a laptop, with pinned versions. Every number in the repo is generated by code, never written by hand or by a model.

**Transferability.** The method generalises to other regulated detection problems: underwriting, sanctions, trust and safety. The dataset does not, and the repo says so.

**Safety.** Public data only. No employer data, no client internals, no confidential material. Experience is described at the level of decisions and reasoning, in the decision log, with industries named rather than companies.

## Non-goals

**Confirmed exclusions.** Decided, not open.

- **Not a fraud detection product.** No deployment, no serving layer, no claim of production readiness.
- **No real-time benchmarking.** The dataset is static and small, so ingest throughput, concurrency and freshness cannot be measured. No claim is made about them. See Assumptions.
- **No production experience claimed.** The framing throughout is: built to test where graph stops paying for itself.
- **No GraphRAG or knowledge graphs.** A different architecture and a different problem.
- **No recommendation engine.** A legitimate graph use case, out of scope here, noted in the README.
- **No HIPAA or FedRAMP mapping.** No health or US federal data is involved. Noted in governance with one line on why regime fragmentation matters to cross-border fintech.

**Not yet defined.** Treat the list above as the confirmed exclusions only.

- Second dataset, orchestration framework, and continuous integration are all deferred rather than ruled out. See Roadmap.

## Hypothesis and pre-registration

**To be committed as `01-the-bet/pre-registration.md` before the first result is generated. Dated, and never edited afterwards.**

**H1.** Graph-derived features added to a tuned gradient-boosted baseline will capture the majority of the available detection lift.

**H2.** A graph neural network will not beat that arm by enough to justify its modelled cost per decision at a sub-one-percent take rate.

**H3.** All arms will degrade after the dataset's known regime shift, and the simpler arms will degrade less.

**What would change my mind.** H2 is rejected if the GNN beats the graph-features arm on precision at the review-queue size by more than five percentage points, sustained across at least three consecutive held-out time steps, with the cost difference still leaving a positive margin under the modelled cost per decision.

**Kill criteria for the build.**

- If no arm beats the plain tabular baseline, the finding is that this dataset carries its signal in node features. That is published, and the GNN arm is not extended.
- If the three arms fall within the noise band of each other, the finding is that the comparison is underpowered on this data. What would be needed to separate them is specified rather than guessed at.
- If the pre-registration cannot be committed before results exist, the comparison is abandoned and the repo ships as a method write-up only.

## Success metrics

Cost figures are **modelled, not disclosed**, and are labelled as such wherever they appear.

| Metric | Baseline | Target | Measurement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detection lift, by arm | Tuned tabular model | Reported, not targeted | Precision at review-queue size k, on held-out time steps |
| Cost per decision, by arm | Rules arm | Reported, not targeted | Modelled compute and storage per scored entity, assumptions stated |
| Degradation after regime shift | Pre-shift performance | Reported | Performance by time step, shift marked |
| Leakage | Zero | Zero | Automated test fails the build if any future information enters training |
| Explainability coverage | n/a | 100% of flags | Every flagged entity emits an evidence chain |
| Reproducibility | n/a | One command, clean clone | Pipeline runs end to end with pinned versions |

**Red lines.** Numeric and blocking.

- No result is published without a leakage test passing.
- No arm is compared on a different split, feature preparation or operating point.
- No number appears in the repo that was not produced by code.
- No figure is presented as disclosed when it is modelled.

## Solution overview

### The escalation ladder

The organising argument. Each rung is only climbed once the one below has been measured and plateaued.

1. Rules and thresholds.
2. Aggregate features in the existing model.
3. Graph-derived features into the existing model: hop distance to a confirmed bad actor, shared-attribute degree, beneficiary fan-in, cluster size and formation speed.
4. Graph algorithms proper: community detection, centrality, link prediction.
5. Graph neural networks.

### Experiment arms

| Arm | Method | Rung |
|---|---|---|
| A | Tuned gradient-boosted trees on node features only | 2 |
| B | Arm A plus graph-derived features | 3 |
| C | Community detection, unsupervised, for ring discovery without labels | 4 |
| D | Inductive GNN node classification | 5 |

Arm A is tuned with the same budget and the same search method as Arm D. An untuned baseline invalidates the entire comparison and is the most common way results of this kind mislead.

### Query shape test

The same two workloads run on an embedded graph store and on a columnar store:

- **Traversal:** multi-hop neighbourhood expansion from a seed entity.
- **Aggregation:** high-cardinality grouping over transaction events.

This measures **query type, speed and cost**, not real-time performance. Configuration and hardware are stated. Results are not generalised.

### Explainability layer

Every flagged entity emits a subgraph and a plain-language reason. A precomputed language-model summary sits alongside, generated offline and committed as data, with a separate check on whether the summary matches the evidence. Where a method cannot produce an evidence chain, that is recorded as a limitation of the method.

## Data requirements

**Primary dataset.** A published, labelled transaction graph of roughly 203,000 nodes and 234,000 edges across 49 time steps, with approximately 2% labelled illicit, 21% labelled licit and 77% unlabelled.

That label distribution is the point. It reproduces the real condition of financial crime detection, where confirmed outcomes are rare, late and biased toward what was already caught, without needing to be simulated.

**Licensing.** Datasets are **not committed to the repository**. A loader script fetches them, and the README records source, licence and any restriction on redistribution or commercial use. Verify terms before publishing.

**Labelling protocol.** A one-page document covering how a human confirms a ring, how late-arriving labels reconcile against earlier scores, and how proxy labels are marked as proxies wherever used.

**Golden set.** Confirmed cases accumulate into a golden set. Human corrections become new golden rows, following the pattern used in the AI strategy repo. Starting size and target size are both stated, and the gap is flagged rather than hidden.

**Leakage.** Treated as a test, not an assumption. See Requirement 2.

## Evals and guardrails

**Eval stack.** Layered, following the pattern in `ai-evals`:

- Automated checks on split integrity, feature provenance and metric computation.
- A separate held-out time period that no arm was tuned against.
- A human rubric for evidence chains: is the reason true, sufficient, and actionable by an investigator.

**Autonomy split**, following the pattern in the AI product strategy repo. Although nothing here is deployed, the split is specified because it is the decision a real system forces:

- **Auto:** scoring, ranking, evidence-chain generation.
- **Human approval:** any action that would block or restrict an account.
- **Never automatic:** closing an account, filing a report, or any irreversible action. No component both decides and executes.

**Bounds in code**, following the pattern in `pm-os-agent`: caps on runtime and on any model spend, with limits set in code rather than described in prose.

## Risks

Ranked, with a confidence score and what would move it.

### Problem space

1. **The dataset does not represent payments fraud** (confidence 7/10 that this limits transferability). It is a cryptocurrency transaction graph with anonymised features, not customer payments. *What would move this:* adding a payments-semantic second dataset behind the same loader.
2. **The anonymised features hide what the model is using** (6/10). Interpretability claims are weaker when feature meanings are unknown. *What would move this:* reporting evidence chains from graph structure, which is interpretable, rather than from node features.
3. **Label bias toward previously caught fraud** (8/10 that this is present). Any model trained on these labels partly learns the previous detection rules. *What would move this:* the unsupervised arm, which uses no labels at discovery time.

### Solution space

1. **Weak baseline invalidates everything** (7/10 risk if not controlled). The most common failure in published comparisons. *Mitigation:* equal tuning budget, recorded in results. Requirement 3.
2. **Cost model is invented** (9/10 that it will be challenged). *Mitigation:* assumptions stated, sensitivity range published, figures labelled modelled.
3. **Micro-benchmark is attackable** (7/10). Anyone who runs these engines professionally will find a hole in a throughput claim. *Mitigation:* claim restricted to query shape, configuration stated, no generalisation.
4. **Scope creep past the deadline** (6/10). *Mitigation:* v1 ships after the evaluation stage with charts in the README if the front end is not ready.
5. **Library and API drift** (5/10). Graph ML and static site tooling move quickly. *Mitigation:* pin versions on day one, thin end-to-end slice first.

## Trade-offs, assumptions and open questions

**Build or buy.** An embedded graph store is used rather than a server-based one, to keep the repository static and runnable from a clean clone. This trades away the features of a managed graph platform for reproducibility, which is the right trade for a lab and the wrong one for production. Stated rather than assumed.

**Real-time.** The dataset is static and small. Ingest throughput, concurrency and freshness are not testable here and no claim is made about them. The lesson from production experience, that a workload turned out to be aggregation rather than traversal, is recorded in the decision log as lived experience, separate from what this repo reproduces.

**Data governance and cataloguing.** Lineage is emitted as a structured artefact rather than managed in a catalogue tool. In an enterprise setting this is where a catalogue with lineage support belongs, and the design anticipates that boundary.

**Data sovereignty.** Cross-border payment graphs and data residency pull against each other: the edges with the most signal are often the ones that cross jurisdictions, and residency rules constrain where they may be joined. A note covers this tension, together with the role of access logging in proving who could see what. This is the most original piece of the governance set and it comes from production experience, not from the dataset.

**Compute.** Everything runs on a laptop CPU at this data size. No claim is made about behaviour at a scale where it would not.

**Open questions.**

| Question | Why it matters | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Does the GNN arm need a heterogeneous graph to be a fair test? | A single node type may understate what graph ML can do. Answering it costs time | Open |
| What is the right review-queue size k for precision at k? | It sets the operating point, and an arbitrary k makes the headline metric arbitrary | Open, needs a stated rationale before results |
| Should the cost model use published cloud list prices or a simple unit model? | List prices look rigorous but age badly. A unit model is clearer and more honest | Open |
| Is the unsupervised arm in v1 or v2? | It answers the hardest question in the domain, and it costs most of a day | Open, decide after the thin slice |

## Roadmap

**Phase 1, Foundations.** Thin end-to-end slice: data loaded, one metric produced, one chart rendered. Versions pinned. Pre-registration committed. *Kill criterion: if the slice is not working by the end of the first session, drop the GNN arm and ship a method write-up.*

**Phase 2, The ladder.** Arms A and B, tuned equally, evaluated on a time-aware split with the leakage test passing. *Kill criterion: if Arm B does not beat Arm A, publish that and do not build Arm D.*

**Phase 3, The expensive rung.** Arm D, with the same evaluation. The cost model and the query shape test. *Kill criterion: stated in the pre-registration.*

**Phase 4, The argument.** Static front end, threshold explorer, evidence chains, governance notes, decision log.

**Phase 5, Later.** Unsupervised arm, second dataset, orchestration with lineage, continuous integration, adversarial tests for cold start and camouflage.
